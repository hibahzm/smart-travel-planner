# Tokyo, Japan

## Overview
Tokyo is Japan's sprawling capital and one of the world's most captivating megacities. A place where ancient temples stand beside neon-lit skyscrapers, Tokyo offers an extraordinary blend of tradition, innovation, and sensory overload. With a population of over 13 million, it ranks among the safest and most efficient cities on the planet.

## Getting There
Tokyo is served by two major airports: Narita International Airport (NRT), roughly 60 km east of central Tokyo, and Haneda Airport (HND), which is closer to the city and connected by express trains in under 30 minutes. The Narita Express (N'EX) reaches Tokyo Station in 53 minutes. Japan Rail Pass holders can use the N'EX for free on their first day.

## Getting Around
Tokyo's metro and JR rail network is legendary. The Tokyo Metro and Toei Subway cover virtually every neighbourhood. An IC card (Suica or Pasmo) makes tapping in and out effortless. Taxis are safe but expensive; Google Maps works flawlessly for navigation. Cycling is popular in residential districts and bike-share schemes are expanding.

## Neighbourhoods
**Shinjuku** — entertainment, nightlife, and the sprawling Kabukicho red-light district; also home to Shinjuku Gyoen National Garden.
**Shibuya** — the famous scramble crossing, youth fashion, and department stores.
**Asakusa** — Tokyo's most traditional district, centred on Senso-ji Temple, the city's oldest Buddhist temple, and the Nakamise shopping street.
**Akihabara** — the global capital of electronics and anime/manga culture.
**Harajuku** — cosplay, quirky fashion, Takeshita Street, and the serene Meiji Shrine amid a forested park.
**Ginza** — upscale boutiques, galleries, and the Tsukiji outer market nearby.
**Roppongi** — international nightlife, Mori Art Museum, and Tokyo Tower views.
**Yanaka** — a rare survival of old Edo-era streetscapes; artisan shops and shitamachi atmosphere.

## See
- **Senso-ji Temple (Asakusa)** — Tokyo's most visited temple; spectacular Kaminarimon gate and five-storey pagoda.
- **Meiji Shrine (Harajuku)** — forested Shinto shrine dedicated to Emperor Meiji; peaceful despite its location in a 70-hectare forest in the city centre.
- **teamLab Borderless / Planets** — world-famous immersive digital art museums.
- **Tokyo National Museum (Ueno)** — the world's largest collection of Japanese art, housing over 110,000 artefacts.
- **Tsukiji Outer Market** — even post-relocation, the outer market retains its vibrant sushi breakfast culture.
- **Shibuya Sky** — rooftop observatory with 360° views over the city.
- **Imperial Palace East Gardens** — free public gardens within the imperial palace grounds.
- **Odaiba** — futuristic waterfront island with the teamLab museums, Palette Town, and a replica Statue of Liberty.

## Do
- Attend a sumo tournament (January, May, September at Ryogoku Kokugikan).
- Participate in a traditional tea ceremony in Hamarikyu Gardens.
- Take a day trip to Nikko's ornate Tosho-gu Shrine complex or the temples of Kamakura.
- Visit a cat café, owl café, or hedgehog café in Shibuya or Shinjuku.
- Explore the Golden Gai quarter in Shinjuku — 200+ tiny, character-filled bars each seating fewer than 10 guests.
- Shop for vintage and second-hand fashion in Shimokitazawa.

## Eat and Drink
Tokyo has more Michelin stars than any other city on Earth (over 200 starred restaurants). But equally compelling is everyday food:
- **Ramen** — each Tokyo shop has its own shoyu, shio, or tonkotsu house recipe.
- **Sushi** — from ¥100 conveyor-belt kaiten-zushi to omakase counters costing ¥30,000+.
- **Yakitori** — grilled chicken skewers, best eaten under the train tracks near Yurakucho station.
- **Izakaya** — Japanese gastropubs; order edamame, karaage, and cold Sapporo beer.
- **Convenience stores (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart)** — legitimately excellent food at any hour; onigiri, sandwiches, and hot snacks rivalling many restaurants.
- **Depachika** — food halls in the basements of department stores (Isetan in Shinjuku, Mitsukoshi in Ginza) are a destination in themselves.

## Sleep
Tokyo accommodation ranges from ¥2,500 capsule hotels to ¥100,000-per-night luxury suites at the Park Hyatt (setting of Lost in Translation) and the Aman Tokyo. Mid-range business hotels (Dormy Inn, Daiwa Roynet, APA) offer clean rooms from ¥8,000–14,000. Traditional ryokan stays can be found in Asakusa and offer futon beds, yukata robes, and multi-course kaiseki dinners.

## Practical Info
- **Currency**: Japanese Yen (JPY). Cash is still widely used despite growing card acceptance.
- **Language**: Japanese. English signage is excellent in tourist areas and transport hubs.
- **Safety**: Exceptionally safe; violent crime is virtually non-existent.
- **Best time to visit**: Spring (late March–early May) for cherry blossoms; autumn (October–November) for foliage. Summers are hot and humid; July–August sees festivals (matsuri) and fireworks.
- **Budget**: Mid-range travellers spend ¥10,000–18,000 per day (USD 65–120). Luxury is world-class but prices reflect it.
- **Etiquette**: Remove shoes when entering homes and many traditional restaurants. Speak quietly on public transport. Never tip — it can be considered rude.
